\section{Abstract}
{
\bf
\looseness=-1
Training on model-generated data risks \emph{model collapse}, while much
in-context learning (ICL) relies on \emph{induction heads}. We run
10-generation fit$\to$sample$\to$refit pipelines (8 seeds) on a few-shot task
that only in-context induction can solve. They first appear to collapse
abruptly: with fully synthetic data 51 of 72 later models fail and none
recovers. This is mostly a training artifact: retraining first failures
without early stopping recovers 4 of 6, and removing duplicate sequences,
which our resampling introduced, leaves 1 failure in 27. What remains is
gradual: without duplicates, half the accurate models lose the classic
induction head yet still solve the task, less accurately (0.85 vs.\ 0.997),
unlike models trained on real data. Marginal symbol statistics miss this,
though synthetic pools are structurally corrupted from generation 1.
}
\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{Keywords:} induction heads; model collapse; mechanistic interpretability
\end{quote}

\section{Introduction} \label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
Models are increasingly trained on earlier models' output.
\citet{shumailov2023curse,shumailov2024ai} show that recursively fitting
generation $n$ to samples from generation $n{-}1$ erodes the tails of the
learned distribution; this is measured through output statistics, while the
model's internals are little studied. We study the \emph{induction head}: a
previous-token head feeding a later head that attends to what followed an
earlier occurrence of the current token and copies it.
\citet{olsson2022incontext} argue it underlies much ICL, and
\citet{singh2024needs} study its abrupt formation on a synthetic few-shot
task, which we adopt because per-sequence relabelling forbids memorising an
exemplar$\to$label table. ACDC \citep{conmy2023towards} and training-data
attribution \citep{chen2026mechanistic} motivate our diagnostics, though we
implement only simplified versions. \textbf{What does recursive training do to
the induction circuit, and how much of the apparent damage is real?}

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Task and model.} Each sequence shows 4 distinct exemplars ($K{=}64$
symbols), 3 times each in random order, each with a label ($L{=}8$) drawn
afresh per sequence; the query exemplar's label is the target. Symbol IDs
replace Omniglot images, a deliberate simplification, as the mechanism does not
depend on the encoder. Pools hold 20{,}000 sequences; validation and test sets
(800 each) come from the true distribution. A causal transformer with 2 layers
$\times$ 4 heads, $d{=}64$. \emph{Base}: loss on the query label only.
\emph{Extended}: auxiliary heads also predict the next symbol and its label, so
it can generate whole sequences. \textbf{Pipeline.} Generation 0 trains on real
data; generation $n$ trains from scratch, from the same initialisation, on a
pool in which a fraction $p$ is sampled from model $n{-}1$ and the rest is fresh
real data. The base model can only resample a real context's \emph{label}; the
extended model samples whole sequences, so errors can compound. Pools for
generations $\geq$1 are with-replacement resamples ($\approx$63\% distinct),
and training stops early on validation loss (patience 30/40). We run 10
generations: extended at $p\in\{0.25,0.5,1\}$, base at $p{=}1$, 8 seeds each (5
with independent real-data pools). \textbf{Checks.} We (i) retrained the first
failure of each failing run without early stopping; (ii) ran controls ($p{=}0$
with and without resampling; $p{=}1$ without duplicates; 3 seeds); and (iii)
analysed every model: its best head's \emph{induction score} (attention the
query puts on the labels that followed earlier occurrences of its symbol;
random baseline 0.12), head and layer ablations, and training-pool structure
(label consistency, repeat counts), against marginal symbol KL (noise floor
$8{\times}10^{-4}$ nats).

\section{Results}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{As first run, collapse looks abrupt and absorbing}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pools}a). At $p{=}1$ the extended model fails (test accuracy
$<$0.5) in 51 of 72 later generations, in 7 of 8 seeds, and none recovers;
counts are 23/72 at $p{=}0.5$ and 8/72 at $p{=}0.25$ (Supp.\
Table~\ref{tab:fail}). The base variant fails in 2 of 63 in one seed, the first
on a pool still 99.99\% valid; another base run failed at generation 0 on clean
data. \textbf{Early stopping manufactures failures} (Fig.~\ref{fig:main}b).
Failed extended models reach training accuracy 0.95 while validation accuracy
peaks at 0.26, and patience stops training after 31--58 epochs. Retrained
without early stopping, 4 of 6 first failures reach at least 0.94, 1 reaches
0.83 after $\sim$190 epochs, and 1 (base) stays failed (Supp.\
Table~\ref{tab:retrain}). \textbf{Duplicates create the cliff}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:main}a). Without duplicates the same three seeds at $p{=}1$ fail
1 of 27 times instead of 22 of 27, declining gradually (generation 9: 0.84,
0.39, 0.96); real-data controls never fail (0 of 54). \textbf{The circuit is
replaced, not just lost} (Fig.~\ref{fig:main}c). Failed models (n=91) have no
head scoring $\geq$0.30, and ablating layer 1 changes nothing. Accurate models
(n=299) average 0.83 for their best head (92\% in layer 1) and lose accuracy if
either layer is ablated. Without duplicates, however, 11 of 21 accurate later
models lack a classic head, against 9\% on real data and 0\% in base (Supp.\
Table~\ref{tab:alt}); across all
runs, such models average 0.85 accuracy vs.\ 0.997 and their layers still
matter (ablation drops 0.61, 0.72): a different attention-based mechanism, not
memorisation. Switches go both ways (8 vs.\ 8 of 251 pairs). \textbf{Marginal
drift is weak, and pool structure does not explain failure}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pools}b). At a model's first failure, symbol KL is a median
$3.9\times$ its floor ($p{=}1$), yet from generation 1 only 16\% of synthetic
sequences are fully valid (repeat counts right in 18\%). Pools from 24 healthy
generation-0 models do not separate the 3 that fail at generation 1 (AUC
0.44--0.68).

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Most of the abrupt collapse we first measured is a training artifact.}
Duplicate sequences make memorising the pool easy, and early stopping on
validation loss fires during the memorisation phase that precedes a delayed
transition to generalisation, so a failure under this protocol is not reliable
evidence that the circuit was lost. Because failed models also generated the
next generation's data, the absorbing failures at $p{=}1$ may largely be
prematurely stopped generators propagating; we did not test later failures.
\textbf{What survives is gradual and mechanistic.} Without duplicates, fully
synthetic training changes which solution training finds: half of the accurate
models lose the classic layer-1 induction head and keep a weaker attention-based
mechanism. Here the circuit is thus replaced, not by memorisation but by a less
accurate mechanism; this rests on 3 early-stopped runs, so a rerun with fixed
budgets and no duplicates is needed. \textbf{Retractions.} Marginal statistics
are nearly blind, but our earlier hypothesis that structural corruption drives
failure is unsupported: it is large yet unrelated to which generation-1 models
fail. The base variant behaves as the brief predicted; its failures began on
near-clean pools, so are seed-dependent training failures.
\textbf{Limitations.} (i) Only first failures were retrained. (ii) Three
no-duplicate runs; the 300-epoch retrain also stretches the learning-rate
schedule. (iii) The induction score detects only the classic pattern; the
alternative mechanism is uncharacterised. (iv) Seeds 0--2 share a real-data
pool. (v) A symbolic task and a tiny model.

\section*{Supplementary Material}

\subsection*{S1. Setup, provenance and reproducibility}
\textbf{Configuration.} $K{=}64$, $L{=}8$, 4 exemplars $\times$ 3 repeats
(12 context pairs, sequence length 25); 2 layers $\times$ 4 heads,
$d_\text{model}{=}64$, $d_\text{ff}{=}256$; AdamW, learning rate $10^{-3}$,
weight decay 0.1, batch 128, 100 warm-up steps with cosine decay, gradient
clipping 1.0; at most 100 (base) or 80 (extended) epochs with early stopping
on validation loss (patience 40 and 30); 20{,}000 training sequences per
generation, 800 validation and 800 test sequences from the true distribution.
Seeds set initialisation and sampling randomness; seeds 0--2 share one
real-data pool and validation/test set (data seed 0), seeds 3--7 each use
their own (data seed = seed). Training ran on mscluster CPU nodes as Slurm
array jobs; no GPU was requested.

\textbf{Provenance.} All 41 collapse runs (4 conditions $\times$ 8 seeds plus
3 controls $\times$ 3 seeds) completed with 10 generations. Original runs:
jobs 62521 (no-collapse), 62535 (base collapse), 62527 and 62798 (extended
collapse and ablations; in the first submission, 6 of 9 tasks crashed at
start-up with a \texttt{FileExistsError} from parallel tasks racing to create
\texttt{results/collapse/}, wrote no results, and were resubmitted unchanged as
62798; the race is fixed in the code). Follow-ups: 63092 (controls), 63093 and
63094 (extra seeds), 63086 (retraining), 63237 (per-generation circuit and
pool analysis; an earlier job, 63085, covered only the original 12 runs and is
superseded). Generation 0 of each original collapse run reproduces the
matching no-collapse run in test accuracy (6 of 6). The first 18 runs predate
the per-generation pool and best-head logging; their pool statistics and
circuit measurements come from \texttt{experiments/analyze\_circuits.py} run on
the saved checkpoints and dataset snapshots. Commands are in
\texttt{README.txt}.

\textbf{Pilot observation (not part of the reported results).} With
4{,}000-sequence pools, some initialisations (and a pool with 0.4\% label
noise) memorised the training set instead of learning induction even without
collapse; 20{,}000 sequences removed this in pilot tests. Those logs were not
retained, so this only motivates the pool size.

\subsection*{S3. Not yet done}
The decisive experiment has not been run: all generations trained with a fixed
budget and no early stopping, on pools without duplicates, for every condition
and 8 seeds (configured in \texttt{configs/v2\_*.yaml}, job group \texttt{v2};
see \texttt{README.txt}, Section 6h). Until it is, the gradual mechanism
replacement in the main text rests on three runs, and the size of any
\emph{residual} collapse effect under a sound protocol is unknown. If it has
been run, the main text must be revised to match. Also open: retraining later (not just first)
failures to test the propagation explanation; characterising the alternative
mechanism the ablation results point to; and the gradient-influence proxy
(\texttt{src/attribution.py}), which has not been applied to the collapse runs.
