Large language models are increasingly trained on data that itself contains
model-generated text, raising the risk of \emph{model collapse}: recursive
training on synthetic data can progressively erode a model's grasp of the
true data distribution. Separately, mechanistic interpretability has shown
that much in-context learning (ICL) is implemented by \emph{induction
heads}, a specific two-head attention circuit. We ask whether model collapse
specifically degrades this circuit, or whether accuracy loss (if any) is
better explained some other way. We train small transformers on a synthetic
few-shot symbolic ICL task designed so that only genuine induction --
never memorisation -- can solve it, verify that induction heads emerge as
expected, then run a multi-generation collapse pipeline in which each
generation trains partly or wholly on the previous generation's own
outputs. We track task accuracy, synthetic-data distributional drift, and
a suite of circuit-level diagnostics (a task-grounded induction score,
previous-token-head score, attention maps, embedding geometry, and a
simplified causal head-ablation sweep) across generations. [TODO: one to

\section{Introduction} \label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
As more of the text on the internet is itself generated by language models,
future models risk being trained, directly or indirectly, on their own
(or their predecessors') outputs. \citet{shumailov2023curse} and
\citet{shumailov2024ai} formalise this as \emph{model collapse}: training
generation $n$ on data sampled from generation $n{-}1$'s outputs, repeated
recursively, causes the learned distribution to progressively lose
low-probability (tail) information and contract toward its modes, yielding
increasingly homogeneous outputs. This has mostly been studied through
downstream statistical quality metrics (perplexity, diversity), leaving
open a more mechanistic question: \emph{which internal circuits break down,
and how, as a model is trained on its own output over successive
generations?}

We focus on one of the best-understood circuits in transformer language
models: the \emph{induction head}, a two-head mechanism (an earlier
\emph{previous-token head} feeding a later \emph{induction head}) that
implements an in-context match-and-copy operation, and which
\citet{olsson2022incontext} argue underlies a large fraction of ICL.
\citet{singh2024needs} study exactly how and when induction heads form,
using a synthetic few-shot symbolic task in which exemplar-label
associations are randomised \emph{per sequence}, so the task is solvable
only via in-context induction, never by memorising a global
exemplar-to-label mapping. We adopt this task as our testbed, train small
transformers on it, and confirm the expected induction-head phase
transition before subjecting the training process to Shumailov-style
recursive collapse.

Because our task's per-sequence relabelling rules out memorisation as an
escape hatch by construction, any accuracy the model retains as its
training data collapses \emph{must} reflect a still-functioning induction
mechanism, and any accuracy it loses cannot be masked by a fallback to
memorisation. This lets us cleanly ask our central question: \textbf{does
model collapse degrade the induction circuit itself, and if so, how} --
does attention become noisier (rising entropy), does the previous-token
head or the induction head degrade first, does the discovered causal
circuit shrink or become redundant, and does this track the synthetic
data's own distributional drift?

\subsubsection{Background}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Induction heads.} \citet{olsson2022incontext} identify induction
heads via a distinctive attention pattern (attending to the token that
previously followed a repeat of the current token) and a matching causal
role in ICL. \citet{singh2024needs} study the training dynamics of induction
head \emph{formation} on a controlled synthetic task, showing it emerges as
a discrete phase transition and depends on data properties like context
"burstiness." We reuse their task design (Method, below) as the substrate
for our collapse experiments.

\textbf{Model collapse.} \citet{shumailov2023curse,shumailov2024ai} show
that recursively fitting a model to its own (or a prior generation's)
samples causes distributional collapse, with partial data replacement
(mixing real and synthetic data) slowing but not eliminating the effect.
We adopt their "fit $\to$ sample $\to$ refit" recursion directly.

\textbf{Circuit-level tools.} \citet{conmy2023towards}'s ACDC automates
discovery of the minimal causal subgraph responsible for a behaviour via
iterative edge ablation; \citet{chen2026mechanistic} trace which
\emph{training examples} cause specific interpretable circuits (including
induction heads) to emerge, via influence functions. We draw on both at
small scale -- a per-head causal ablation sweep, and a lightweight
gradient-influence proxy -- to connect our collapse results back to
specific heads and specific data properties, rather than stopping at
aggregate accuracy.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Task.} Following \citet{singh2024needs}, a sequence consists of
$m = n_\text{classes} \times B$ context pairs (exemplar, label) followed by
a query exemplar; the target is the query's label. Each sequence shows
$n_\text{classes}$ distinct exemplars, each repeated $B$ times
("burstiness"), and assigns each exemplar a label drawn \emph{freshly and
uniformly at random for that sequence only} (\citet{singh2024needs}'s
\texttt{fewshot\_relabel}), so solving the task requires finding an
exemplar's earlier occurrence in-context and copying its label -- never
memorising a global exemplar$\to$label table. \textbf{Scope simplification:}
exemplars are integer symbol IDs from a vocabulary of size $K$, not Omniglot
character images as in \citet{singh2024needs} -- the image encoder is an
implementation detail for perceptual realism that the induction mechanism
does not depend on, and a symbolic vocabulary is far easier to control
(exact repeat structure) and interpret.

\textbf{Model.} A small causal transformer (2 attention layers, mirroring
the previous-token-head $\to$ induction-head circuit's minimal depth; 4
heads/layer; $d_\text{model}=64$). A \emph{base} output head predicts only
the query's label (the base task). An \emph{extended} model adds two
auxiliary heads predicting, at every context position, the next symbol and
its corresponding label -- turning the model into a full generative model
of sequences, which is what lets it \emph{autoregressively sample entire
new sequences} for the collapse pipeline (Section~\ref{collapse-method}),
not just labels.

\textbf{Collapse pipeline.}\label{collapse-method} At generation 0 we train
on real (freshly sampled, ground-truth) data. At generation $n\geq1$, a
$p$ fraction of the training pool is synthetic data \emph{sampled from
generation $n{-}1$'s model}, and $1{-}p$ is fresh real data (partial- vs.
full-replacement, per \citet{shumailov2023curse}); we do not accumulate
synthetic data across generations, matching the single-hop
fit$\to$sample$\to$refit recursion. Critically, \emph{how} sampling works
differs by variant, which is the crux of our design: the \textbf{base}
model can only output a label, so "sampling" means taking a real context
and resampling only its query label from the model's predictive
distribution -- context structure can never drift this way, so collapse is
expected to be weak, and we report this as an honest baseline rather than
omitting it. The \textbf{extended} model autoregressively rolls out an
entire synthetic sequence (context symbols, their labels, the query, and
its label), so its own errors on the auxiliary predictions can compound
across a sequence and skew the symbol/label distribution fed to the next
generation -- this is the variant expected to show real collapse. Every
generation's exact training pool and resulting model checkpoint are saved,
so any generation can be re-inspected after the fact. Every condition is
run with 3 seeds (model-init held fixed within a collapse run, varied
across replicates), reported as mean $\pm$ std across generations: during
development we found the base variant's training is itself seed-sensitive
(Discussion), so trusting a single run per condition would be a weak basis
for claims about collapse. All training was run on the Wits mscluster HPC
cluster (CPU, Slurm job arrays parallelising across the 6 conditions
$\times$ 3 seeds).

\textbf{Interpretability.} We track, per generation: task accuracy/loss/
perplexity; symbol and label usage entropy and KL divergence of each
generation's synthetic pool against generation 0's true distribution
(the direct analogue of \citeauthor{shumailov2024ai}'s distributional
contraction); a \emph{task-grounded induction score} -- attention mass the
query position places on the label positions that immediately follow
earlier in-context occurrences of the query's own symbol, i.e. the exact
positions a correct induction circuit must copy from; a
\emph{previous-token score} for the earlier layer; attention-map
visualisations; and symbol-embedding PCA. As a stretch goal, we implement a
simplified, single-pass ACDC-style sweep (zero-ablating each attention head
and measuring the resulting accuracy drop, individually and per-layer) to
identify which heads are causally necessary and whether that changes across
generations, and a lightweight, single-checkpoint TracIn-style
gradient-dot-product influence proxy (inspired by, but not a faithful
reimplementation of, \citealp{chen2026mechanistic}) relating a data-side
label-self-consistency measure to circuit degradation. Both stretch tools
are explicitly exploratory/approximate; see Discussion.
